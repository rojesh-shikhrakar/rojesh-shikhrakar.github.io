## Recommend, never decide

Twenty years of public-sector automation point to one design choice that separates success from scandal. Where AI ranks, drafts, summarises and flags _before_ an officer decides, the record is strong: faster complaint routing, better audit selection, damage maps within days of a flood. Where an AI score _becomes_ a penalty, a denial or an arrest, with nobody accountable in between, the result has been court rulings and settlements.

> **Type A:** documents → AI → summary, ranking or draft → **officer decides** → service
>
> **Type B:** citizen data → AI → **automatic outcome** → citizen gains or loses a benefit, payment or freedom

Every program is built to help institutions design Type A systems on purpose. I have written more on the evidence in [AI for Government: Where It Works, Where It Breaks](/insights/ai-technology/ai-for-government-recommend-not-decide).

## Five questions every team learns to ask

1. **Who signs?** A named officer owns every consequential outcome. If the answer is "the system," stop.
2. **What happens when it's unsure?** There is a visible "uncertain → human" path, and it gets used.
3. **Which source is authoritative?** Answers cite the current circular or regulation, with superseded versions retired.
4. **Can a citizen contest it?** People can see why they were flagged and appeal.
5. **Who evaluates the vendor?** Independent testing on your own data, including the edge cases, is budgeted before signing.

## From one workflow to national capability

Programs can be pitched at the level your institution works at:

- **Workflow level:** pick one painful process, estimate the hours at stake and design the review points. 100,000 applications a year at 30 minutes each is 50,000 staff-hours; halving review time returns the work of about a dozen officers.
- **Institution level:** build a use-case portfolio, an impact-assessment template, an incident protocol and procurement rules that secure data ownership, audit rights and an exit path.
- **National level:** assess Nepal's AI stack across talent, data, compute, hosting, models, applications, security, governance and procurement, and decide where to **build, adapt, buy or partner**. A small state does not need to own every layer. It needs enough control, bargaining power and resilience over the ones that matter.

## Why this matters for Nepal

The pieces already exist: satellite imagery, a disaster authority that has worked with AI-assisted damage mapping, and a large body of government documents in Nepali waiting to be made searchable. What's missing is not a better model. It is clean local data, clear ownership of decisions, sovereign control over sensitive workloads, and officials who know how to question what the model tells them. Building that capability is the work of these programs.
